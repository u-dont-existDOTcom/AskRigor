How to research: general cautions

Before you trust a single study

  • "No conflicts of interest" means none were declared. When researchers compare authors' declarations with public lists of company payments, they usually find payments that weren't declared. How many depends on what you count (section 1).
  • Many findings don't hold up when someone repeats the work. In large projects that repeated published studies, between about a fifth and two-thirds of findings came back, depending on the field and how "came back" was judged. The effects were usually smaller the second time (section 2).
  • Headlines and press releases often claim more than the study found, and sometimes the paper's own summary does too (section 3).
  • Not every study gets published, and some change what they measure. Positive results are more likely to reach print (section 4).
  • These are reasons for care, not for distrusting science. One study is rarely the last word. A finding becomes dependable when independent teams find it again, in larger studies planned in advance, and when careful reviews bring all the studies together (last section).

1. Money ties that aren't declared

Journals ask authors to declare payments from companies. Some countries publish what companies paid doctors, so researchers can compare the two.

  • If every payment counts, most paid authors left something out. A review of 27 such comparisons, mostly from the US and mostly in surgery, found that in the typical study about 8 in 10 authors with listed payments (median 81%) had a declaration that didn't fully match the payment records. About 4 in 10 (median 43%) had declared none of the listed payments. The studies ranged from 42% to 99% (El-Rayess 2020). The review's authors caution that the studies used different definitions, often didn't check whether a payment related to the paper or fell under the journal's rules, and treated the payment lists as complete. So these numbers may overstate the problem.
  • Recent US examples. Among US doctors who were first or last authors of trials in two leading US journals in 2017 and had received company payments, 81% (86 of 106) had not declared at least one payment from a company working in the paper's broad area (Baraldi 2022). Among US first and last authors of drug trials in three leading journals (2016-2018), 80% (198 of 247) hadn't declared some or all of their payments (Tau 2019).
  • Counting only payments clearly tied to the study gives much lower numbers.
    • Danish doctors who wrote drug-trial reports: 13% hadn't declared ties to the trial's sponsor or the drug's maker, while 43% hadn't declared ties to some drug company (Rasmussen 2015).
    • Industry-funded trials in three leading journals: in 8% (10 of 125), the first or last author hadn't declared personal payments from the sponsor (Tau 2019).
    • Two plastic-surgery journals: 79% of authors had at least one mismatch, but about 4% of papers were judged to hide a tie that counted as a conflict of interest (Ruta 2026).
  • The rules and thresholds change the answer. In a French orthopaedics journal in 2017, 201 articles had a French author with interests over €1,000 in France's public register. In 94% of them, at least one such interest was left out; for larger interests, the share was 49% (Erivan 2021). In a Japanese breast-cancer guideline, 17 authors were paid above the guideline's own threshold, and 1 of them had made no declaration (Saito 2019).
  • The payment lists aren't complete either. A US cancer society compared its authors' declarations with the US government's payment database. On average, 37% of what an author had declared wasn't in the database, and 47% of an author's database payments hadn't been declared (Garrett-Mayer 2020).
    • The US database covers only US clinicians, and only payments from makers of drugs and devices. It misses, for example, many complementary medicines (Baraldi 2022).
    • The UK list names a doctor only with their consent: 55.5% of payments were named in 2015 (Mulinari 2018).
    • In Germany naming is voluntary: named payments covered 27% of the money in 2015 (Stoll 2020).

So, is it 80%? About 80% is a fair figure for one thing: the share of paid US authors whose declarations miss at least one payment of any kind. It is not the share of studies hiding a conflict that matters for their results. Counting only payments clearly tied to the study, the figures above run from about 4% of papers to 13% of authors, and they vary with the field, the country and the journal's rules.

What you can do:

  • Read the funding and conflict-of-interest statements, and read "none declared" as a claim.
  • Notice who paid for the study and who ran the analysis.
  • For US doctors, Open Payments shows what drug and device makers reported paying them. Make sure you have the right person, because names repeat.
  • A payment doesn't make a study wrong. It is one reason to look harder at how the study was done.

2. Findings that don't hold up when repeated

Two words first. Reproducing a study usually means getting the same result from the same data and code. Replicating it means running it again with new data and seeing whether the result comes back.

  • Psychology: 100 studies from three journals were repeated. 97% of the originals had statistically significant results; 36% of the repeats did, and 39% were judged to have replicated. Effects were about half as large (Open Science Collaboration 2015).
  • Economics and social-science experiments: 11 of 18 economics lab experiments (61%) came back, at 66% of the original effect on average (Camerer 2016). 13 of 21 social-science experiments from Nature and Science (62%) came back, at about half the original effect (Camerer 2018).
  • Cancer biology (lab studies): A project set out to repeat 193 experiments from 53 high-profile papers. It could repeat only 50 from 23, often because methods and data weren't reported well enough (Errington 2021a). For positive effects, 40% replicated by most of the project's measures, and the typical effect was 85% smaller than the original (Errington 2021b).
  • Biomedical lab research in Brazil: of 45 experiments with valid repeats, between 20% and 44% replicated, depending on which of five criteria was used. This is a preprint, not yet published in a journal (Brazilian Reproducibility Initiative).
  • Clinical research: 45 very highly cited studies claimed a treatment worked. Later studies contradicted 7 of them (16%) and found smaller effects for another 7 (16%); 20 (44%) were replicated, and 11 (24%) weren't seriously tested again (Ioannidis 2005). In one leading medical journal from 2001 to 2010, 146 of 363 articles (40%) that tested an established practice found it no better than a lesser alternative (Prasad 2013).
  • Same data, same code:
    • Economics: 33% of 67 papers could be reproduced from the authors' files without asking them, and 49% of the 59 testable papers with their help (Chang & Li 2015).
    • The journal Science: for a random sample of 204 papers, data and code could be obtained for 44%, and the findings reproduced for 26% (Stodden 2018).
    • A recent check of 110 economics and political-science papers: over 85% were fully reproducible from the authors' files, but coding errors turned up in about a quarter, and about 70% of results held up when re-analysed in other reasonable ways (Brodeur 2024).
  • What researchers say: In a 2016 online survey of 1,576 researchers by Nature, more than 70% said they had tried and failed to reproduce another scientist's experiment, and more than half had failed with one of their own. Nature notes the survey probably drew people already concerned about the issue (Baker 2016).

So, are most studies not reproducible? That is too strong as a blanket statement. These projects tested selected studies, often high-profile ones from a few fields, and judged success in several ways. What they show is that in several fields a large share of tested findings, often around half, didn't clearly come back. Those that did were usually weaker.

What you can do:

  • Ask whether independent teams have found the same thing.
  • Look for a systematic review rather than a single study.
  • Give more weight to large studies whose plans were registered in advance.
  • Expect the real effect to be smaller than the first report.

3. Claims that grow on the way to the headline

  • Press releases: 462 health press releases from 20 leading UK universities were checked against the studies behind them. 40% gave advice that went beyond the study, 33% turned a correlation into cause and effect, and 36% applied animal results to people. When a press release exaggerated, the news coverage usually did too (Sumner 2014).
  • The paper's own summary: 72 trials had a main result that wasn't statistically significant. In 58% of them, the abstract's conclusions had "spin": wording that made the treatment look beneficial anyway, or drew attention away from the main result. 18% had spin in the title (Boutron 2010). A review of 35 studies found spin in trials, observational studies and reviews alike, at rates that varied widely (Chiu 2017).

What you can do:

  • Go past the headline and the abstract to the results.
  • Find the main outcome the study set out to measure.
  • Check whether the study was in people, or in animals or cells.
  • Check whether it showed cause, or only a link.

4. Missing studies and switched outcomes

  • Positive results get published more. For 12 antidepressants, the US regulator judged 51% of 74 trials positive, but the published papers made 94% of the trials look positive. 31% of the trials weren't published at all. Overall, the published literature made the drugs look 32% more effective (Turner 2008).
  • Many trials stay unpublished for years.
    • Of 635 completed trials funded by the US National Institutes of Health, 46% were published within 30 months. After a median of 51 months, a third were still unpublished (Ross 2012).
    • Across 51 US academic medical centres, results were shared for 66% of 4,347 trials, and within two years for 36% (Chen 2016).
    • Of 4,209 trials that US law required to post results within a year, 41% did so on time and 64% at any time (DeVito 2020).
  • Outcomes get switched.
    • In 102 Danish trials, 62% had at least one main outcome changed, added or dropped between the protocol and the paper (Chan 2004). A review of 20 studies found 40% to 62% where reports were compared with protocols (Dwan 2013).
    • Of 67 trials in five top medical journals, 58 (87%) needed a correction letter for misreported outcomes. On average, each trial added 5.4 outcomes it hadn't declared in advance (Goldacre 2019).

What you can do:

  • For a trial, look up its registry entry (for example ClinicalTrials.gov).
  • Compare the main outcome planned there with the one reported, and see whether results were posted.
  • Prefer reviews that searched for unpublished trials.

Careful, not cynical

  • Much research holds up. In the repeat projects above, between about a fifth and two-thirds of tested findings came back. In clinical research, 44% of the most-cited claims were confirmed by later studies.
  • The research that found these problems also shows how to judge a finding: independent repeats; larger studies planned in advance; results posted even when they disappoint; data and code shared; and reviews that collect all the evidence.
  • These numbers come from particular samples, often high-profile journals in a few fields. They describe research in general. They can't tell you whether a particular study is right; for that, look at how it was done.
