Piliostigma reticulatum leaves: Health benefits and toxicity

Scientific name: Piliostigma reticulatum (DC.) Hochst. | Family: Fabaceae

Piliostigma reticulatum is a medicinal plant native to West Africa, including Senegal, where it is commonly known as Nguiguis (Wolof; spelling varies).

Its leaves are traditionally used for diarrhea, gastrointestinal complaints, wounds, inflammation, and infections.

The important distinction is that traditional medicinal use and laboratory evidence do not establish clinical effectiveness or safety in humans. Most reported benefits come from phytochemical studies, laboratory experiments, and animal research.

1. Potential health benefits

Potential benefitScientific interpretation
Antioxidant activityPolyphenols and flavonoids may help neutralize free radicals in laboratory assays.
Antibacterial activityLeaf extracts may inhibit certain bacteria under laboratory conditions.
Anti-inflammatory activitySome experimental findings suggest anti-inflammatory potential.
Antidiarrheal effectsTraditional use and preliminary experimental evidence suggest possible gastrointestinal benefits.
Wound healingTraditional application may be supported by antimicrobial and antioxidant properties, but human evidence is limited.
Blood sugar regulationPreliminary research on the plant suggests possible metabolic effects; leaf-specific human evidence is insufficient.

These effects depend on the extraction method, dose, and chemical composition of the leaves.

2. What scientific studies have demonstrated

Several studies provide more specific evidence:

  • Anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects (2021): A study of P. reticulatum leaves found antioxidant activity and inhibition of protein denaturation, an experimental indicator of anti-inflammatory potential. PubMed
  • Antimicrobial effects: Laboratory studies found activity against organisms including Staphylococcus aureus, Escherichia coli, and Candida albicans. Activity varied considerably by extraction solvent. Sudan Journal of Medical Sciences +1
  • Pain relief: A Senegalese study reported reduced dental pain following the use of a leaf decoction as a mouth rinse. The investigators emphasized that further clinical studies were necessary. PubMed
  • Antimalarial potential (2022): Methanolic leaf extracts showed dose-dependent antiplasmodial activity in infected mice. This does not establish effectiveness against malaria in humans. Springer Nature Link

3. Toxicity and safety

Acute oral toxicity findings in animals

Freeze-dried leaf preparation17,000mg/kg reported LD50
Methanolic leaf extract5,000+mg/kg reported LD50

LD50 is the dose estimated to cause death in 50% of experimental animals. These figures come from separate animal studies with different preparations, and are not recommended human doses. PubMed +1

These results suggest relatively low acute lethality for the tested leaf preparations. However, acute toxicity is only one aspect of safety.

Potential concerns include:

  • Long-term toxicity: Insufficient evidence about repeated daily consumption and effects on the liver, kidneys, or other organs.
  • Digestive effects: Tannins may cause gastrointestinal discomfort or interfere with mineral absorption when consumed excessively.
  • Pregnancy and breastfeeding: Insufficient reproductive and developmental safety data.
  • Drug interactions: Interactions with prescription medicines have not been adequately characterized.
  • Preparation differences: Boiled leaves, powdered leaves, and concentrated solvent extracts can contain substantially different concentrations of active compounds.

Importantly, a separate study found appreciable acute toxicity from a concentrated stem-bark fraction, with an LD50 around 3,000 mg/kg. This should not be interpreted as the toxicity of the leaves, but it demonstrates why results cannot be generalized across plant parts and preparations. afjbs.com

4. Is it safe to drink Piliostigma reticulatum leaf tea?

There is not enough evidence to establish a safe daily human dose, maximum duration of use, or safety during long-term consumption.

A traditional water decoction is also not equivalent to the methanolic extracts used in many experiments. Consequently, animal doses should not be converted directly into instructions for preparing tea.

I would not recommend prolonged daily consumption for medicinal purposes without professional guidance, particularly for children, pregnant people, or individuals taking regular medications.

5. Overall assessment

Conclusion: Piliostigma reticulatum leaves have promising medicinal properties, particularly antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, antimicrobial, and analgesic activity. Available animal studies suggest relatively low acute toxicity for some leaf preparations, but long-term human safety remains uncertain.

One important question: Are you interested in using the leaves as a tea/decoction to drink, as a mouth rinse, or as a powdered supplement? The toxicity assessment differs substantially between these preparations.

Piliostigma reticulatum leaves: health benefits and toxicity

Piliostigma reticulatum, known as Nguiguis in Wolof, is a medicinal shrub or small tree common in West Africa, including Senegal.

Its leaves have promising evidence for pain relief, anti-inflammatory activity, blood-glucose regulation, and antimalarial effects.

The most interesting finding is that a Senegalese clinical investigation actually tested a leaf decoction for dental pain. Most other evidence comes from laboratory or animal experiments.

1. Dental pain relief: particularly interesting

A published study of P. reticulatum leaves from Senegal examined a traditional leaf decoction used as a mouth rinse.

The researchers reported pain relief in:

97%Patients following tooth extraction
78%Patients with periodontal ligament pain syndromes

These are reported response percentages, not placebo-controlled efficacy estimates. The study did not establish how much improvement was attributable to the decoction rather than natural recovery or other treatments.

An accompanying animal experiment also found dose-dependent analgesic activity. pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov +1

2. Antimalarial activity

A 2022 mouse study examined a methanol extract of the leaves in malaria-infected mice.

The highest leaf-extract dose also extended survival compared with untreated infected mice. These results are promising but do not establish a treatment for human malaria. Springer Nature Link

3. Blood sugar regulation

A 2019 rat study used aqueous leaf extract, making this especially relevant to traditional water preparations.

At 200 mg/kg, the extract substantially suppressed the rise in blood glucose after starch or maltose administration. This suggests an effect on carbohydrate digestion or absorption.

That is relevant to post-meal glucose spikes, but effectiveness for diabetes or long-term glucose control in humans remains unestablished. Tropical Journal of Natural Product Research

4. Anti-inflammatory and antioxidant activity

A 2021 study conducted partly with researchers from Cheikh Anta Diop University in Dakar found that methanolic leaf extracts contained flavonoids, tannins and other polyphenols.

The extract inhibited heat-induced protein denaturation, a laboratory indicator sometimes used to screen potential anti-inflammatory compounds. Its activity was near that of diclofenac in this particular assay, but that does not mean it performs like diclofenac in people.

The same study found substantial antioxidant activity in DPPH and ABTS chemical assays. pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov +1

5. Anxiety, sleep and anticonvulsant effects

This is another notable finding.

A 2013 study specifically investigated leaf decoctions in animal models of anxiety and fever. Doses of 35–350 mg/kg produced anxiety-related behavioral changes, including more exploration of the open arms of an elevated maze. ajphs.com

A separate 2009 experiment reported protection against chemically induced convulsions and increased diazepam-induced sleep. The PROTA botanical monograph identifies leaf decoctions as the preparations tested. PubMed +1

These findings justify further investigation, especially for anxiety and seizure mechanisms, but there is no demonstrated human seizure-control benefit.

Toxicity: what has actually been tested?

Acute oral toxicity, dried-leaf preparationLD50 ≈ 17,000 mg/kg

Reported in the Senegalese analgesic investigation; animal testing.

Acute oral toxicity, methanol leaf extractLD50 >5,000 mg/kg

Reported in the 2022 antimalarial mouse experiment.

Both results indicate relatively low acute lethal toxicity in the tested animals, but they are not contradictory: the preparations and experimental conditions differ. LD50 is the dose expected to kill half the exposed experimental animals, not a recommended dose or an estimate of long-term safety. pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov +1

The major remaining safety questions are:

  • Liver and kidney toxicity: repeated-dose safety remains insufficiently characterized for ordinary leaf tea.
  • Pregnancy and reproductive safety: not adequately established. Traditional use during labor is not proof of safety.
  • Neurological effects and interactions: the sedative and anticonvulsant animal findings warrant caution when combining it with sleeping medications or other sedatives.
  • Blood glucose: its activity in rats makes interactions with glucose-lowering medicines biologically plausible, although human interaction studies are lacking.
  • Long-term consumption: neither the acute LD50 studies nor occasional traditional use establishes the safety of drinking a concentrated decoction daily for months.

The plant contains tannins and other bioactive compounds. Concentrated tannin-rich preparations may affect gastrointestinal tolerance and mineral absorption, but these effects have not been quantified specifically for P. reticulatum leaf tea.

Traditional uses worth distinguishing from confirmed benefits

The PROTA medicinal-plant monograph documents leaf use for wound healing, nausea, dysentery, hemorrhoids, headaches, cough, epilepsy, and sedation. These are ethnobotanical observations rather than controlled clinical findings. Pl@ntUse

The evidence is strongest as a rationale for studying topical or mouth-rinse applications, because the dental observations involve human use. For drinking leaf tea, animal findings are encouraging but a clinically effective dose and repeated-use safety profile have not been established.

Overall assessment

Piliostigma reticulatum leaves appear to have genuine pharmacological activity and relatively low acute toxicity in animal experiments. That is encouraging, particularly for dental pain, inflammation, anxiety, and blood-glucose regulation.

However, evidence of low acute toxicity is not sufficient to conclude that daily ingestion is safe. Human liver, kidney, reproductive, and long-term safety data remain important gaps.

The most useful distinction for future research is between a water decoction and a concentrated methanol or alcohol extract. Their chemical composition and effects may differ substantially.
